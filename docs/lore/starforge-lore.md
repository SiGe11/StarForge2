# STARFORGE: WORLD LORE

> The canon for StarForge's story. Everything the game says or shows (text, names, speech, art, the intro) is checked against this file; the rules for writing are in section 13, the Old Language in [old-language.md](old-language.md) and the image and video prompts in [prompts.md](prompts.md) beside it. Nothing here contradicts the game's code, data or README: where a game rule the lore leans on changes, the line here changes with it. Changes to the lore itself are the author's decision.

## 1. In one paragraph
The Empire of the Brightest Star has kept its own peace for seven centuries. Its capital in law is the star itself, where nobody lives. It is governed in the star's name by two hands that may never become one: the Khan, who holds, and the Matriarch, the Holy Mother, who blesses and who elects each Khan. Long before the Empire, its ancestors worked **starfire**, a fire that gave made things a purpose of their own. That fire went out a thousand years ago. Now surveyors on the emptied farm-world of Vesper have found signs of the **Starforge**, the hearth where starfire was first worked. The Muster could not agree what to do with it, the four wings of the Imperial Host landed to "secure the site", two and then two more, and neither the Khan nor the Holy Mother has said a word. Four quarters of one army, under one oath, are fighting over a thing no living person has seen.

## 2. The Bright Age and the Quenching
- **Starfire.** The old magic was never spells or sorcerers. It was a craft of making. A thing forged in starfire kept a **purpose**: a wall that meant to stand, a plough that meant to cut straight, a machine that meant to guard. The Mech's line "Steel does not possess courage. It possesses purpose." is the last living statement of that craft.
- **The Makers.** The smiths who worked starfire. They held that it was sunlight brought down to the anvil. No Maker's name survives, only their work.
- **The Starforge.** The first hearth, from which every other starfire was lit.
- **The Quenching.** In one night every starfire went out. Nine watches were kept over the Starforge that night, and the fire failed during the ninth. Purposed things kept their purpose but lost the power to act on it alone.
- **Three accounts of why**, none proven:
  - *The Luminary:* the fire was lent by the stars and has gone home. It still burns where it always burned. Look up.
  - *The Khan's chroniclers:* it was spent. The last Makers burned it all in a war nobody recorded.
  - *The orders:* the Makers put it out themselves, and the orders swore something that night which they do not discuss.
- **The Long Night.** The name of the present age. There is no magic in it, and nobody alive has met anyone who saw any.

## 3. The Empire of the Brightest Star
- **The capital.** The Brightest Star is a blue-white giant, the one light that stands in the night sky of every world the Empire holds. It has no habitable world and nobody lives there. Sovereignty is held to rest in the star itself, and the Khan and the Matriarch are its two hands.
- **"Given at the Star."** An act under both seals is dated "given at the Star" and binds the whole Empire. An act under one seal is dated from that hand's own seat and binds only that hand's sphere.
- **Founding.** Three centuries of house wars followed the Quenching. The first Matriarch ended them by electing one war-captain over all the others, on condition that he never rule the faith. That bargain is the **Compact**.
- **The Matriarch, the Holy Mother** (now Aveline III). Spiritual head, seated at the **Mother Hall**. Chosen for life by the senior Mothers of the Luminary; the Khan has no voice in it. She elects the Khan and is the only authority that can release a sworn oath.
- **The Khan** (now Temrik IV). Worldly head. Commands the Host, the courts and the treasury. His seat is a stone city still called **the Camp**, because the first Khans ruled from the saddle.
- **The Election.** When a Khan dies, the Matriarch keeps nine nights of vigil and names the successor at vespers on the ninth. The **Muster**, the assembly of house-captains, then swears to him. It has never refused, and the law does not say what happens if it does. The new Khan sails once to within sight of the Brightest Star to be invested, the only time a ruler visits the capital.
- **Limits on the Election.** She elects and never commands. A Khan once invested holds for life and cannot be unseated by her. She may not elect her own kin or anyone in the Luminary's service.
- **The rule of the Compact.** No one holds both hands. Matters of the sword take the Khan's seal, matters of the light take the Mother's, and matters of both must be given at the Star. The Starforge is the first matter in seven centuries that is wholly both.
- **The Imperial Host.** By old custom it is kept in equal wings, so that no captain ever commands all of it. At the founding there were two, **Blue** and **Red**. A later Khan, wary of his own Host, split them into four, adding **Green** and **Yellow**, with the Holy Mother's blessing given at the Star. No captain has commanded more than a quarter since, though old soldiers still say "the two halves". Same establishment, same equipment, same oath, sworn under the Brightest Star: "by the hand that holds and the hand that blesses".
- **The wings follow the houses.** Each house's companies serve one wing by old custom, so whole districts send their sons and daughters to one wing. That is why the Muster's division fell along the wings. Kin in another wing come by marriage across houses; soldiers call them "cousins across".
- **The houses.** They survive as the Muster's house-captains. They hold land and raise companies for the Host, but no house may keep an army of its own: that was the Compact's price.
- **Worlds.** A few dozen settled worlds, a handful of them great. The oldest worlds carry the names of the hours, like Vesper; newer settlements have plain names.
  - **Matins**, the seat-world. The Camp and the Mother Hall stand in the same stone city, facing each other across one square, which neither hand crosses without the other's leave.
  - **Terce**, the garden-world, which feeds the seat.
  - **Harrow**, the printworks world: smoke, guild halls and works-towns.
  - **Sext**, a cold world where the orders keep their oldest holds. The Host keeps no garrison there.
  - **Nones**, a quiet world where starfire was once worked. Its quenchstone was mined out long before the Empire, and nobody lives near the old seams.
- **Travel** is slow: weeks to months between worlds. A letter from Vesper reaches home in a season. The High Watch's ships are faster, and nobody knows how.
- **Money.** People pay in **stars**, the Khan's silver, with the Brightest Star on one face and the reigning Khan on the other; the Host's pay is counted in it. Houses and works settle in printworks credit, drawn on what the works can make. Ore is the real money underneath.
- **The guilds** own and run the printworks and sell to every wing. That is why they stay out of the quarrel, and why a Digger is sworn to the Foundry and not to the war.
- **The courts are split like the hands.** The Khan's courts take land, coin and crime. The Luminary's take oaths, marriages and the dead. A case that touches both goes to a joint bench, which sits rarely and slowly.
- **Reckoning of years.** Dates are counted in years of the Compact, in the Camp, the Luminary and the Host alike.

**Life in the Empire**
- **By world.** The garden-worlds farm, the printworks worlds toil, and the seat-world governs. Everywhere the day follows the hours: work from lauds, home by vespers, and the night for the family and the sky.
- **Names.** A person has a given name, a house and a birth-star: "Ilse of House Varen, under the Wren". Everyone is of a house, by birth or by service. The full name is used only in law and at vespers. Two people under the same star are star-kin.
- **Flame.** The Empire keeps true flame wherever it can: oil lamps and candles in halls and homes alike. Electric light is for works and war.
- **Reading.** Nearly everyone reads. The halls teach the young from the lectors' texts.
- **Leisure.** Star-watching from the rooftops, the oldest pastime. Horse-racing at the Camp's festivals, from the days when the Khans ruled from the saddle. Storytellers' tales of the orders, of knights and cores, which every child knows and no knight confirms.
- **The war at home** is distant and late. News comes a season old, in letters. Most people want their sons and daughters back more than they want the quarrel settled.
- **Temrik IV, the Khan.** A scholar-Khan, chosen from outside the camps: a reader of the chronicles who had never commanded a company. His election surprised the Muster, which swore to him all the same. He is said to read the Annalists' reports himself.
- **Aveline III, the Holy Mother.** Surprisingly young for the veil. She is a theologian, very devout, and plainly in love with the faith; her readings of the High Speech are studied in every hall. She elected Temrik. The Camp's gossip credits her with strong appetites. Nothing has ever been shown, and in public she keeps the discipline of her office without fault, out of respect for what the office stands for.

## 4. The Luminary and the Rite of Stars
- **From one sun to all stars.** The oldest cult was of a single sun, the Makers' sun. When the people spread to other worlds, each under a different sun, the cult widened: every sun is a star and every star a sun. The Quenching finished the turn. The light can no longer be worked, only received.
- **What is taught.** All light is one kindred. Each person is named under a **birth-star**, the star risen at their birth, and keeps its rising as their own feast. The Brightest Star is first among the kindred and holds the Empire. The dead are said to have "gone into the light"; the Luminary declines to say more.
- **The Luminary.** The body of the clergy. The Matriarch at its head, the **Mothers** over the provinces, and under them **celebrants**, cantors, lectors, acolytes and thurifers. Every office of the Luminary is held by women.
- **The halls.** A hall of the Rite is long, built of stone, and open to the sky above the altar through a round **oculus**. Its far end is aligned on the rising of the Brightest Star. A pierced screen hung with lamps and gold-ground star-icons stands between the people and the altar; its doors open only at the Taking of the Light. Behind it burns the **hall-lamp**, the flame every other light in the hall is lit from. There are no seats.
- **The hours.** The day is kept in offices: **lauds** at sunrise to the world's own sun, which is the old sun-cult surviving in one short office; **vespers** at the first star; **compline** before sleep; **vigils** through the night on the eve of a feast. People greet each other by the hours: "good lauds", "good vespers", and after compline "keep the night".
- **Two tongues.** Everyone speaks the Vulgar, at home and on the field net. The **Old Language** (*Hihspæce*), which the Luminary calls the High Speech, is the tongue of the Bright Age, from which the Vulgar grew as Italian grew from Latin. Nobody has spoken it at home for centuries. It is kept for the liturgy, oaths, titles, mottos, acts given at the Star and the Annalists' formal records. The clergy and the orders know it well, and the educated a little. Its grammar, words and set texts: [old-language.md](old-language.md).
- **The Great Liturgy** is sung every ninth night, counted from the nine watches of the Quenching; the other eight nights keep only the hours. It runs from the first star to lauds, in the High Speech.
  - *Music:* chant with no instrument. The cantors hold a low sung drone under the celebrant's line, hand bells mark the parts, and one great bell rings vespers.
  - *Posture:* celebrant and people face the same way, toward the altar and the Star. The people stand for the chant and kneel at the Taking.
  - *The lamps:* each worshipper brings their house-lamp.
  The rite has six parts:
  1. *The first star.* A celebrant holds the mirror-dish under the oculus until it catches the first star. Only then may the rite begin.
  2. *The Darkening.* As the chant begins, every lamp in the hall is put out one by one, the people's with them. The hall waits in the dark under the open oculus.
  3. *The Readings.* Lectors read in the Old Language from the three books:
     - the **Book of Makings**: sayings of the Bright Age, unsigned;
     - the **Annals of the Long Night**: the record since the Quenching, which the Annalists still add to;
     - the **Book of Mothers**: the Matriarchs' rulings and sermons.
  4. *The Litany of Names.* The cantor sings the names of the stars and then the names of the year's dead, and the people answer each name. Processions with lamps and swung censers go round the hall.
  5. *The Taking of the Light.* At the night's height the screen's doors open. A lamp is lit from the hall-lamp, and its flame is passed out lamp to lamp, celebrant to people, until the hall is lit. Nothing is eaten and nothing is asked for: the light is received and given on.
  6. *The Dismissal.* Chant and long silences go on until the world's own sun rises. Lauds ends the rite, and the people go home into daylight carrying their lamps lit.
- **Star-water.** Water that has stood a night under the open sky. Used to bless people, standards, thresholds and the dying.
- **Vestments.** A long white linen alb, girded with a cord whose colour shows the office: plain for acolytes and thurifers, red-brown for lectors and cantors, gold for celebrants, white for Mothers. Over these a wide sleeveless mantle in the colour of the season, stiff with gold thread and worked with star-points. For vespers and processions, a great hooded cope clasped at the breast. Hands are covered with a silk shoulder-veil when carrying the mirror-dish. Every cleric is veiled, and the veil lengthens with the office: a short head-veil for an acolyte, to the shoulder for a lector or cantor, to the waist for a celebrant, to the hem for a Mother. The Matriarch alone is veiled in white from crown to floor. Each office carries its own light: an acolyte a taper, a celebrant a hand-lamp, a Mother a staff topped with a lamp. A Mother also wears a tall closed crown set with a single stone.
- **The vessels.** The **mirror-dish**, a shallow dish of polished metal, times the rite by catching the first star. Censers are swung by thurifers. A bowl of star-water stands at the door, and the hall-lamp burns behind the screen.
- **Colours.** Gold for feasts, white for ordinary time, black for the Night of the Quenching. Blue, red, green and yellow were laid aside when the wings went to war and will not be worn again until the Host is one.
- **The year's feasts.**
  - *The Night of the Quenching:* black vestments. Every lamp is put out one by one during the chant, the hall-lamp last, and the hall is left dark and silent until lauds.
  - *The Kindling,* the next night, is the year's joy, in gold. The Mother of each province strikes the year's one new flame from flint, the only flame anyone may make from nothing. Every hall-lamp of her province is lit from hers, and every house-lamp from its hall.
  - *The Compact Day,* a feast of state: the one day the Khan and the Holy Mother appear together, each on their own side of the square on Matins.
  - *The Rising:* the night the Brightest Star stands highest over Matins. The Matriarch herself sings the Great Liturgy in the Mother Hall.
  - *Each person's birth-star rising,* their own feast.
- **In the field.** Every wing carries field celebrants. They wear the white alb over plain field dress, with no iron and no weapon: the only white figures on a battlefield, and by custom nobody aims at them. Vespers is sung at the first star in every camp, in the same words, sometimes within earshot.
- **Why the Mothers lead.** "The light is born, not made." Since the Quenching the light can no longer be forged, only borne and given, and the Luminary holds that bearing is a mother's office. So the whole clergy, from the Matriarch to the youngest acolyte, are women. It is the mirror of the other hand: only a man may be Khan, as only a woman may be Mother.
- **The rites of a life.**
  - *Naming:* a newborn is named at vespers under their birth-star and touched with star-water.
  - *Coming of age:* a youth keeps a night's vigil alone and greets their world's sun at lauds.
  - *Marriage:* vows at the first star, two lamps lit from one.
  - *The last light:* the dying are given star-water, and a lamp is put out when they go.
- **Two gestures.** On entering a hall, the fingers are laid over the eyes, then lifted: "I was dark; I see." At the first star, at a death and when taking an oath, the right hand is raised, palm open and up, toward the sky: receiving, never grasping.
- **At home.**
  - *The house-lamp,* lit at the Great Liturgy. If it goes out, a neighbour gives the flame back; it is never relit from nothing. One night in nine the house is dark: the night its lamp is carried to the hall.
  - *A star-window:* a small round opening facing the Star's rising, the house's own oculus.
  - *Lauds at the threshold:* the household greets its world's sun at the door each morning.
- **The clergy's lives.** Girls enter a hall-house as acolytes at about twelve, learn the Old Language and the chant, and rise office by office; a late vocation is welcome and rare. Clergy live in the hall-house beside their hall. A Mother's seat is the great hall of her province, and the Mother Hall on Matins is the greatest. Celebrants and the lower offices may marry. A Mother gives up marriage when she takes the crown, and the Matriarch is wed to her office.
- **Washing.** Wrongdoing is washed in star-water at lauds by a celebrant, quietly and privately. The Rite keeps no confession and no list of sins; only an oath, once broken, needs the Holy Mother.
- **No pilgrimage.** The Brightest Star stands in every sky, and the Luminary teaches that no place is nearer to it than any other. The Khan's voyage at his investiture is an act of state, not devotion.
- **Purposed things in daily use.** A few Bright Age things still do their work: a plough that cuts straight, a well-wheel that will not stop, a door that keeps out the cold. Families hand them down. When one fails, the village keeps a vigil for it as for a death.
- **Dissenters the Luminary tolerates.**
  - *The Sun-keepers:* country folk who keep only lauds, to their own world's sun, as the oldest cult did. Tolerated and gently mocked.
  - *The Watchers of the Ninth:* a lay movement that keeps vigil every night, waiting for the fire to come back. They claim no tie to the Ninth Vigil, and the order has never acknowledged them.

## 5. The disruption
- **Teal.** Proper name **quenchstone**. It is found only on worlds where starfire was once worked, and scholars take it for the residue the fire left. It holds no magic. It is simply the best feedstock known for the printworks that raise a Foundry in minutes. Its seams lie in facing pairs, as if cast from one pour.
- **Vesper.** Named for the hour: from this world the Brightest Star is the evening star. A temperate world seeded with the old stock: birch, spruce, wolf, fox, eagle. Smallholders farmed it for centuries. A hundred years ago the Muster cleared them off for ore concessions (**the Clearance**). The concessions failed, the land went back to birch and wolves, and the field walls and house corners are what the farmers left. Many soldiers in every wing are their great-grandchildren.
- **The Seam Survey.** Host surveyors mapped Vesper's twin seams and reported that they all run back toward one origin. Their conclusion: the Starforge is on Vesper.
- **The split Muster.** Asked for its counsel, the Muster divided four ways, and the division fell along the wings.
  - **Blue Wing, the Kindlers.** Find it and relight it. The purposed things are failing one by one, a people has a right to its inheritance, and to work the light again is the fullest communion there is.
  - **Red Wing, the Keepers.** Find it and seal it cold under both seals. The fire went home, and taking it down a second time is theft. Whoever holds a lit Starforge needs neither Khan nor Mother.
  - **Green Wing, the Bearers.** Find it and carry it to Matins, whole and unlit, under both seals, so that the two hands decide together. Custody, not judgement.
  - **Yellow Wing, the Returners.** Find it and give it back to the orders, who guarded it before there was an Empire, whatever they swore on the night of the Quenching.
  - Each suspects the worst of the others. Red believes Blue's captains want the fire for themselves; Blue believes Red means to break the Starforge and call it piety. The Bearers are called a cart waiting for a driver, and the Returners are asked why the orders should be given back what they once failed to keep.
  - **Natural pairs, often broken.** Keepers and Returners would both keep it from use; Kindlers and Bearers both want it found whole. Those are the usual pairs on a field, and every other pairing has happened too. An alliance is agreed between captains the night before a field and seldom outlasts a season.
  - **The landings.** Blue and Red landed first. Green and Yellow came a season later, each claiming to settle the matter, and made it worse.
- **The Silence.** Either leader could end the war with a sentence. Neither has spoken. The common explanations:
  - Each waits for the other, because one hand ruling alone on a matter of both breaks the Compact.
  - The Khan owes his seat to the Matriarch and will not speak before she does; she will not speak because any ruling defines doctrine on the fire for ever.
  - They agreed on silence together and are letting the wings exhaust themselves.
  - They disagree with each other, and the war below is theirs by proxy.
- **In law.** No wing has been named rebel. All four are lawful hosts of the Empire, and officially there is no civil war, only "a disputed custody".

## 6. The war on Vesper
- **Shape.** No front line. Dozens of small engagements, each in a fresh area of operations along a seam, each wing following the ore toward an origin none has reached.
- **The irony everyone has noticed.** The seams are the only map to the Starforge, and every wing digs them up to print tanks. The war eats its own map.
- **The Commander.** A field officer of a wing with a service record like any other. Deliberately blank. In the game as it stands the posting is to the Blue Wing, and in a single engagement the officer across the field is Red; in an engagement of two wings against two, any wings may stand on either side. The game's text takes no side in the wings' quarrel, so the posting can become a choice later.
- **The enemy who knows you.** Before the split, the Host's **Annalists** kept a dossier on every officer. Each wing took its share of the archive and all of the habit. The officer across the field (graded Recruit, Veteran or Commander) is served by a staff that studied you in peacetime and amends the file after every engagement. It sees only what its own units see, because the orders in orbit share nothing with any staff.
- **The oath.** Every soldier swears the Host-oath. Fear breaks some of them, and the broken go to ground behind their own base. The first thing a deserter does is cut the leads of the iron's masts, so as to hear no more orders: in the ranks a deserter is "gone quiet". Aircrews do not desert: there is nowhere to set down among the broken.
- **Squads on the field net** are called by birth-star names (Wren, Lark, Heron and the rest), never by number.
- **The iron.** Every soldier of the Host fights in **the iron**: a full suit of plate with no helm, the eyes behind a slit in the breastplate and the shoulders in the wing's colour. Soldiers say "in iron" and "taking the iron". The two masts on its back carry the **field net**: the squad's reports go out on it and the Commander's orders come in.
- **Faceless, like the Makers.** In the field nobody's face is seen. The Host holds that this is the Makers' way: they left their work and no faces. A soldier's face is seen at vespers and at burial.
- **Handed down.** Iron is issued, mended and issued again. Inside each breastplate are the names of those who wore it before, scratched one under another. A soldier adds their own on taking it, and nobody scratches one out.
- **One Host under the paint.** Every wing's iron is the same iron. Under the blue, red, green or yellow is the old Host grey, and the shoulders were painted at the split. Iron taken from another wing's dead is painted over and worn. Soldiers do not talk about whose names are inside it.
- **The Ironclad.** A walker of the house wars, older than the Compact. In the three centuries after the Quenching no house was a lawful host, so no order answered a house, and the houses built their own: a walker in a knight's shape with no core and nothing to say. The Host kept the pattern after the Compact and brought it out again for Vesper.
- **The Ironclad's riders.** Riding a walker is a craft of a few old house lines, kept alive through seven centuries of peace and taught by an elder of the line. A rider still wears the iron, and the walker's saddle takes it.
- **The knights' contempt** for the Ironclad dates from the house wars. A knight calls it a kettle, a stilt-house or a pretender, never by its name.
- **Capes.** A soldier who proves themselves takes a second, stricter oath before a knight and receives the knight's cape in the wing's colour, with the Holy Mother's palmette in the lower corners, sprinkled with star-water at vespers. Its trim is bronze, and gold for those who prove themselves again. No soldier in a cape is known to have run.
- **Diggers.** Guild labour, sworn to the Foundry and not to the war. They flee guns, never the guild.
- **The Biolab.** The breeders' craft is a science of the Long Night with no starfire in it. Its stock is what the Clearance left on Vesper: feral cattle, reed-bed frogs, wasps, spiders. The Luminary permits it on a narrow argument: a bred creature swears nothing, so it can break nothing. Soldiers put it more simply: they never desert. Most knights call it a purpose forced on a living thing, which is the one thing the Makers never did. Soldiers name the creatures after their stock: frogs, wasps, spiders and the cattle. Field celebrants will not sprinkle a creature with star-water.
- **The flame-troops.** Scorchers carry fire as a weapon in a faith that turns on a fire that went out. Celebrants keep their distance, and soldiers call the flame-troops lamplighters, not kindly.
- **Aircrews.** They keep vespers by the first star seen from the air, which is often before anyone on the ground has seen it.
- **Marksmen** are drawn by custom from the Clearance families, who know Vesper's fields from their grandparents' telling.

## 7. The knightly orders
- **Origin.** Older than the Empire and older than the Luminary. They descend from the guards of the Makers and hold what remains of the Makers' greatest work. They keep the hours by their own, older use.
- **Cores.** At the heart of every Mech sits a **core**, a purposed mind forged in the Bright Age. No one can make another. Frames, legs and weapons are modern and interchangeable; the core is the Mech. Since the Quenching a core can think, remember and advise, but cannot move a limb without a sworn human hand.
- **No core is shown.** It rides sealed in a reliquary inside the torso. Only its knight and the serjeants who carry it home have seen one.
- **The count.** Each order keeps its number of cores secret from the Host and from the other orders. Everyone knows the number is falling.
- **The frames.** Frames, legs and weapons are forged in the orders' own holds. No printworks of the Host makes them.
- **The swearing.** A candidate keeps nine nights of vigil in the order's hold and at the ninth watch lays a bare hand on the core. Only men are sworn as knights. A knight is sworn for life and renounces house and kin at the oath; he gives up his house name and is known by his birth-star instead, as Anselm Wrenstar, born under the Wren. Few die old. Serjeants, Vigilants and the orders' other offices may be held by women or men.
- **Two voices.** By the orders' teaching, the calls, reports and verdicts are the knight's, and the quiet quoted sayings are the core's ("The enemy has experience. I have memory."). By the same teaching only the knight hears the core. What the Commander hears is always the knight's voice, passing it on. A core takes on something of the temper of the hand it is sworn to. Sceptics hold there is no second voice, only knights reciting their order's book of sayings. Knights who are asked say "we" and change the subject. Whether a core has a name of its own, no knight will say.
- **A core sworn again.** A core sworn to a second knight sometimes brings the first knight's sayings back in the new knight's mouth. The orders call that memory. The sceptics call it the same book read twice.
- **What the Luminary teaches.** A purposed thing has a purpose, not a voice. Some field celebrants are less sure than the Mother Hall.
- **Camp tales about the cores** (told in every wing, proven by nobody):
  - A knight of the Ninth Vigil gave a saying on the net in a form of the High Speech older than any book the orders keep.
  - A Mech whose knight was killed in the saddle stood upright for the length of a vespers chant before it fell.
  - Two knights of the Wardens, on fields a world apart, gave the same quiet line in the same hour, word for word. The sceptics' best evidence: one book.
  - A knight of the Brazen Host, feverish in a field hospital, spoke the core's sayings in sleep. Whose they were, the orderlies could not say.
- **The High Watch.** The orders keep ships over any world where the Host fights. Orbit is neutral and has been since before the Compact.
- **The Gantry Right.** The orders swear to neither Khan nor Mother nor wing. They are bound to answer: any lawful host that raises a gantry is owed one knight and one core, once, for that field. Because the Silence leaves every wing lawful, the orders must answer all four, the Returners no more and no less than the rest. On a field of two wings against two that means four gantries and four Mechs, and the cores are spent accordingly. If either leader named a wing rebel, its gantries would go unanswered. Every wing knows it. Before the Compact no house was a lawful host, so no house was answered.
- **The gantry** is a cradle and a beacon: the cradle the Mech is landed in and mended on, the beacon calling the High Watch until the drop. The seven minutes between a gantry standing and the drop are the passage of a High Watch ship over the horizon.
- **One per war.** A knight counts a war from the lit gantry to the decided field. One core is all an order will stake on it. A fallen Mech is not replaced, and a fallen gantry cancels the summons.
- **Fitting-out.** The Vigilants of the High Watch see the whole field and fit the Mech in the last seconds before the drop, to answer what the enemy has brought. By agreement among the orders, the Mechs on a field are seldom fitted alike, so no knight can claim the other was favoured.
- **Field names.** A knight gives each fitting a field name, such as "the Third Lantern", and lets it go when the field is decided.
- **Gifts.** A host may give its knight better servos, armour or an uplink, made in its own works. A knight accepts gifts and asks for none. The frame under them stays the order's.
- **After the fall.** The order's serjeants walk the field afterwards to bring the core home. They take the core and leave the frame. Host scavengers strip it, which is forbidden and done anyway: a piece of a Mech is a soldier's trophy and a soldier's shame. Not every recovered core wakes again. This war is spending cores faster than any in the record, which each wing cites as proof of its own case.
- **A halved oath.** When knights of one order answer opposing gantries, they fight in earnest. The survivor carries the fallen standard home, and no grudge is permitted.
- **The verdict.** Knights are the keepers of oaths, and custom gives them judgement over the oathbroken. Whether a knight hunts deserters or spares them is the knight's own conscience. The orders do not rule on it. By repute the Sable Oath never spares and the Emberguard seldom punishes; both orders deny any rule.
- **The licensed lance.** Only an order may sanction a weapon built to kill a knight. So each host's single Mech-Hunter rides under an order's flag, crewed by its serjeants, one lance at a time. The orders' reasoning is older than the Empire: "a knight who cannot be killed is a tyrant". Nobody now says which tyrant was meant.
- **The lance's two purposes.** It is meant for the knight who forgets the oath, and for the cheap copies of knights the houses made, which the orders would see gone from every field. An Ironclad is the lance's business as much as a Mech is.
- **Its making.** The lance was the Sable Oath's work, built on a principle the Makers left in the orders' keeping, and the other orders put their seals to it after. A host gets one in two steps. It petitions an order; when the grant comes, a sealed pattern and a crew of serjeants come with it, and the host's Armory forges the lance to the pattern. Each lance is granted once. When it falls, the host waits on a new grant.
- **The serjeants** are former soldiers of the Host who left the iron for an order's oath. They know every side of the field. A crew may draw a lance licensed by the very order whose knight stands across the field. That is the hardest duty a serjeant draws: those who have done it are honoured above the rest and do not speak of it.
- **The Ironclad** is the houses' imitation, kept by the Host: a small walker with no core and nothing to say (section 6).

Reputation is hearsay. In the field a knight's own temper and pride decide, not the order.

**Titles are offices, not ranks.** A Castellan keeps an order's hold, a Seneschal its household, a Marshal its knights in the field, a Knight-Warden a single core. A Paladin is a sworn champion, a Knight-Errant answers the gantries nobody else will, and a Vigilant has served on the High Watch.

## 8. The Starforge
- **What is said.** A hearth no larger than a village smithy, with a fire that needed no fuel. Other accounts say an anvil, a crucible, a hammer, or a place and not a thing. No description is older than the Empire.
- **What each party fears.** The Kindlers, that it will be found and wasted. The Keepers, that it will be found and used. The Bearers, that one wing will decide what belongs to both hands. The Returners, that anyone but its old guardians will hold it. The Luminary and the Camp, that it will be found by one hand. The orders have not said.
- **What a few surveyors suspect.** Twin seams from one pour, all running back to one origin: the teal may not point to the Starforge. It may *be* the Starforge, broken at the Quenching and scattered as slag. If so there is nothing to find, and every wing is feeding it into its foundries by the tonne.
- **What the Ninth Vigil knows.** Unstated. They were there.
- **The name.** Soldiers of every wing call the whole campaign "the Starforge", as in "I did two seasons in the Starforge".

## 9. Soldiers' lore
Soldiers of every wing share all of it: they were one Host until the split.
- **The Reckoning.** A Vesper folk-tale carried by the Clearance families: the beasts of the fallow land keep the oldest oath of all, and a commander who wins by foul means does not keep the win. The soldiers run, and the animals have the last word. The Luminary neither teaches nor denies it. No officer admits to believing it.
- **The Night of the Quenching on Vesper.** On that night every camp puts out its lamps one by one, within sight of each other, and no shot is fired until lauds. No order is given and no officer admits to it. Soldiers sometimes meet between the lines in the dark, trade letters for kin across and share food. Officers know and say nothing.
- **Who is in the iron.** Women serve as well as men, though fewer of them. The iron hides everyone the same, and in the field nobody knows who is inside.
- **Rations.** Vesper feeds the camps: feral cattle from the Clearance's herds, fish from the reed-beds, birch-sap in spring. A beast shot near camp feeds a company.
- **Letters** come by slow ship, a season late. The dead's letters keep arriving after them. A company reads its letters at vespers.
- **Discipline.** A soldier's fault is washed in star-water at lauds before the whole company. The shame is the punishment.
- **The wounded** go to field hospitals kept by guild surgeons, neutral like the Diggers. They treat every wing's wounded, and every wing leaves them alone.
- **No followers.** Vesper is a war zone: no traders, no families, no squatters. Only the Host, the guild and the orders' serjeants walk it.
- **The fallen** are buried where they fall. Their iron goes back to the Host with their name now among those inside, and the next soldier to wear it reads it.
- **Birth-stars in the field.** A soldier on the feast of their birth-star is spared the night watch by custom. To fall on one's own star's rising is called being "taken at the rising", and is held a good death.
- **"The Ninth Watch"**, the slow march every wing sings in the same words. On quiet nights each camp hears another's verse a beat behind its own. Its refrain: *Nine watches kept and nobody slept; we walk the ninth till morning.*
- **Twins**, the camp game. It is played with teal chips on a board scratched into a plank. The chips are laid in facing pairs, as the seams lie, and each player takes from their own side; whoever leaves a chip without its twin loses it. Officers forbid it because the chips are Foundry feed.
- **The soldiers' words**
  - *in iron*, *taking the iron*: serving; enlisting.
  - *the grey*: the old Host colour under the paint; "we're all grey underneath".
  - *gone quiet*: deserted (masts cut).
  - *a season*: a posting on Vesper ("two seasons in the Starforge").
  - *a cousin across*: kin serving in another wing.
  - *teal*: quenchstone.
  - *the lance*: the Mech-Hunter.
  - *kettle*: an Ironclad (the knights' word, taken up by soldiers).
  - *lamplighter*: a flame-trooper.
  - *frogs, wasps, the cattle*: the Biolab's creatures.
  - *to keep the ninth*: to stay awake to the end of anything.

## 13. Writing rules
For anything the game says or shows. Read this section before writing player-facing text, a name, a line of speech or an art prompt.

**Who speaks, and how**
- **The knight** (the Mech's tagged calls, its arrival, its verdicts on deserters): plain and present, with places and numbers. Says "I", addresses "Commander".
- **The core** (the Mech's quiet quoted lines, which by the orders' teaching the knight passes on): old, certain, restrained and dry. One or two short sentences, sometimes three. Never rants, begs or jokes broadly. British spelling. It may remember the Bright Age; it declines to explain it.
- **Soldiers** (squad reports, alerts): who, where, what. No lore.
- **The game's own voice** (menus, manual, tips): plain modern English. Where it explains how the opponent works it may say "the AI"; headings and flavour use the world's names.
- **The Annalist** (the intro's narrator): a dry archivist briefing an officer. Never says which wing the listener serves.

**Two tongues.** Everything above is in the Vulgar. The Old Language appears only where [old-language.md](old-language.md) allows it, built from its grammar and lexicon and glossed on first use. Knights' calls and soldiers' reports never use it.

**Never answered.** No text confirms or denies anything in section 11. A speaker may refuse to say; nobody may say.

**No side taken.** Nothing the game says in its own voice, or the knight's, or the core's, prefers the Kindlers, the Keepers, the Bearers or the Returners. The orders serve the field, not the quarrel.

**The enemy is another wing of one Host.** Lawful, under the same oath. Never "rebels", "traitors", "heretics" or "aliens". A speaker may hold their skill in contempt; nobody hates them.

**Words**
- Use: starfire, the Makers, purpose and purposed, the Quenching, the Long Night, the Brightest Star, the Host, wing, Kindlers, Keepers, Bearers, Returners, the Compact, the Silence, the Muster, the Luminary, lauds and vespers, star-water, seam, printworks, the Clearance, gantry, core, knight, order, the High Watch, Vigilants, the Gantry Right, serjeants, Annalists, dossier or file, the iron, the field net, the lance, stars (the coin), star-kin, cousins across, years of the Compact, Matins, Terce, Harrow, Sext, Nones.
- The Ironclad is never "a knight" or "a Mech". A knight's lines may call it a kettle, a stilt-house or a pretender; the interface calls it the Ironclad.
- The ore has three names by speaker: **quenchstone** (formal), **teal** (soldiers), **ore** (the game's interface).
- The Mech's rider is a **knight** in the world and may be the **pilot** in the interface.
- Avoid: magic, spell, sorcery (except to deny it), gods, prayers that ask for something (the light is received, not petitioned), Earth's places and dates, "robot" or "program" for a core.

**Numbers that must agree**
- The fire went out 1,000 years ago; 300 years of house wars; the Empire is 700 years old; the Clearance was 100 years ago.
- A Mech stands seven to nine metres. One knight and one core a side, a field.
