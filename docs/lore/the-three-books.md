# THE THREE BOOKS OF THE READINGS

> Companion to [starforge-lore.md](starforge-lore.md) and [old-language.md](old-language.md), and canon like them. At the Great Liturgy the lectors recite from three books, in the Old Language, by heart and in the dark (lore section 4, *The Readings*). This file holds six pages of each. The Old Language is the text. The Vulgar under it is the halls' own translation, which is taught and never read aloud at the rite. The first sentence of each page is glossed word by word, as the set texts are. Every word comes from the lexicon in [old-language.md](old-language.md); the words these pages added are listed there under *Words of the three books*. Changes are the author's decision.

**How a page is read.** A lector recites one page at one Reading. The three books are read in the same order every ninth night: Makings, Annals, Mothers. Nobody holds a book at the rite. The books are kept in the hall-house and learned there.

---

## 1. The Book of Makings (*Makunganas Boke*)

Sayings of the Bright Age, unsigned. No saying has a name over it, and the Luminary holds that none ever had one: the Makers left their work and no faces. The sayings are unnumbered. The halls give each page a heading from its first words.

### Page one: Of the hand and the anvil (*Handtheof and anviltheof*)

> *Sunnathe lighthisem fieldthetö and stantheto anlike givethit, and noght again asketh. Swa workthem giv.*
>
> *Thing anes makethsk, and purposhis anes givethsk. Hit twies ne giv.*
>
> *Purposam which thou self ne holdest, thingtö ne giv.*
>
> *Hamorthe ironthem ne askethit. Handthe askethsk.*
>
> *Workthem merk, makerthem ne. Workthe lasteth; makerthe gangeth.*
>
> *Man which namhisam workthon settethit, workthut taketh.*
>
> *Thingem which purposhisam breakan magethit, ne mak. Purposam which hand breakan moteth, ne mak.*
>
> *Hamortwa, an anvilon: an smiteth, other bideth, and workthe an.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Sunnathe*: sun-the.
- *lighthisem*: light-its-OBJ.
- *fieldthetö and stantheto*: field-the-to and stone-the-to.
- *anlike givethit*: alike gives-it.
- *and noght again asketh*: and nothing back asks.

In the Vulgar:

> The sun gives its light to the field and to the stone alike, and asks nothing back. Give the work so.
>
> A thing is made once, and its purpose is given once. Do not give it twice.
>
> A purpose you do not hold yourself, give to no thing.
>
> The hammer does not ask the iron. The hand asks itself.
>
> Mark the work, not the maker. The work lasts; the maker goes.
>
> Whoever sets his name on the work takes something out of it.
>
> Do not make a thing that can break its purpose. Do not make a purpose that a hand must break.
>
> Two hammers, one anvil: one strikes, the other waits, and the work is one.

### Page two: Of the plough and the furrow (*Ploghtheof and furwatheof*)

> *Ploghthe streght cutteth; furwathe ploghmanas.*
>
> *Wall standan meneth. Whar hit standeth, sum man cheosde.*
>
> *Dorathe cald agen standeth. Which man eke ut standeth, handthe cheoseth which hit closethit.*
>
> *Wheelthe turneth; waterthe drinkeres.*
>
> *Purpos thingtö givethsk; usung handto leavethsk.*
>
> *Plogham ne blam; ploghmanam ask.*
>
> *Fieldthes endem lok, ploghthes fotam ne; and furwathe streght gangeth.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Ploghthe streght cutteth*: plough-the straight cuts.
- *furwathe ploghmanas*: furrow-the ploughman's.

In the Vulgar:

> The plough cuts straight; the furrow is the ploughman's.
>
> A wall means to stand. Where it stands, somebody chose.
>
> The door stands against the cold. Who else stays outside, the hand that shuts it chooses.
>
> The wheel turns; the water is the drinker's.
>
> The purpose is given to the thing; the use is left to the hand.
>
> Do not blame the plough; ask the ploughman.
>
> Look to the field's end, not the plough's foot, and the furrow goes straight.

### Page three: Of the apprentice (*Lernertheöf*)

> *Maister namhisam workthon ne settethit; lernerthe hit knowethit, and ne writethit.*
>
> *First workthinam hold. Hit thee shal leran what handanthin forgetant.*
>
> *Ironthem ne hast; hit thee bifore bidede.*
>
> *Lernerthe smiteth whar maisterthe sheweth, til maisterthe nevre eft sheweth.*
>
> *Maister an which mare ironam spillde thanne thou.*
>
> *Handthem ler, and handthe otherem shal leran.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Maister namhisam*: master name-his-OBJ.
- *workthon ne settethit*: work-the-on not sets-it.
- *lernerthe hit knowethit*: apprentice-the it knows-it.
- *and ne writethit*: and not writes-it.

In the Vulgar:

> A master does not set his name on the work; the apprentice knows it, and does not write it.
>
> Keep your first work. It will teach you what your hands forget.
>
> Do not hurry the iron; it was waiting before you.
>
> The apprentice strikes where the master points, until the master points no more.
>
> A master is one who has spoiled more iron than you.
>
> Teach the hand, and the hand will teach another.

### Page four: Of enough (*Ynoghof*)

> *Fewe thingenem mak, and which beginnest, end.*
>
> *Thing which lokungto makdesk, lokethsk, and noght ne doeth.*
>
> *Hearth which ofermuche fedethsk, smithem burneth.*
>
> *Ynogh mete, ne wane.*
>
> *Ironam leav hemtö which after cumant.*
>
> *Hamorthem stopp whan workthe donen, ne whan armthe werig.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Fewe thingenem mak*: few things-OBJ make.
- *and which beginnest, end*: and which (thou) beginnest, end.

In the Vulgar:

> Make few things, and finish what you begin.
>
> A thing made to be looked at is looked at, and does nothing.
>
> A hearth fed too much burns the smithy.
>
> Enough is a measure, not a lack.
>
> Leave iron for those who come after.
>
> Stop the hammer when the work is done, not when the arm is weary.

### Page five: Of the morning (*Morgentheöf*)

> *Sunnathewith ris; hit ne bideth.*
>
> *Which morgenin beginnethsk, daeglightwith endethsk.*
>
> *First light nan manas; ech fieldön anlike.*
>
> *Sunnathem thresholdbi gret; hit er givde.*
>
> *Dewthe ech fieldön lieth, and namam ne asketh.*
>
> *Shorta shadwin work.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Sunnathewith ris*: sun-the-with rise.
- *hit ne bideth*: it not waits.

In the Vulgar:

> Rise with the sun; it does not wait.
>
> What is begun in the morning is finished by daylight.
>
> The first light is nobody's; it lies on every field alike.
>
> Greet the sun at the threshold. It has given already.
>
> The dew lies on every field and asks no name.
>
> Work in the short shadow.

**Notes.** The halls hear the old sun-cult in the morning sayings: the greeting at the threshold is the household's lauds, kept at the door every morning. The book's sayings about the use of things are as old as its sayings about their making; the halls teach that the plough's saying is why a purposed thing is handed down and not owned.

### Page six: Of the work and the fire (*Worktheof and firetheöf*)

> *Sunnathe fieldem ne cheoseth. Makerthe ironhisam cheosethit.*
>
> *Man which wrathin smitheth, thingem maketh which wraththem holdethit.*
>
> *Thingtö purposhisam giv, and hit loos. Hand which workthem ne loosethit, hit spillethit.*
>
> *Mak himtö which after cumeth. Workthes endthem ne shaltit seon.*
>
> *Thingem mourn whan purposhis endeth, ne whan ironhis breaketh.*
>
> *Eventidbi hearththem cover. Hit ne quench, and hit darkin ne fed.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Sunnathe*: sun-the.
- *fieldem*: field-OBJ.
- *ne cheoseth*: not chooses.

In the Vulgar:

> The sun chooses no field. The maker chooses his iron.
>
> Whoever forges in anger makes a thing that keeps the anger.
>
> Give the thing its purpose, and let it go. A hand that will not let go of the work spoils it.
>
> Make for the one who comes after. You will not see the work's end.
>
> Mourn a thing when its purpose ends, not when its iron breaks.
>
> At evening bank the hearth. Do not quench it, and do not feed it in the dark.

**Notes.** This page stays the book's last. Its last saying is read on the Night of the Quenching and on no other night: it is the last thing spoken before the hall-lamp is put out. It makes no claim about the Quenching, and the halls teach that it should not be heard as one. The saying on mourning is the one villages quote when they keep vigil for a purposed thing that has failed.

## 2. The Annals of the Long Night (*Longa Nightes Annalan*)

The record since the Quenching. The Annalists still add to it, and its newest leaves are not read at the rite until the Mother Hall admits them. The first leaf is older than the Annalists, who began at the Compact. Nobody knows whose hand wrote it. Before the Compact the years were counted from the Quenching, as years of the Night. The night of the Quenching itself has no year.

### Page one: The night that has no year (*Nighttheöf which yeerem ne haveth*)

> *Yon nightin Sterresmithethe ofer nine wecchen holdedantsk.*
>
> *Firste wecchethein firethe burnde, swa hit evre burnde. Hit after seven wecchenthaöf noght writethsk. Ninthe wecchethein firethe failde. Which ninthe wecchethem holdedeit, her ne writethsk.*
>
> *Laudesbi ploghantha stille streght cuttedant, and wallantha stille standan mendant. Ech purposed thing purposhisam holdedeit. But nan eft ne sterdesk: ech handam bidede.*
>
> *Yon morgenin sunnathe ech worldon risde, swa hit evre risde. Nan man hittö namam ne havde. Nightthes first daeg nemndesk, and hitfrem Nightthe tellethsk.*
>
> *This leafam annalistantha ne writedantit. Which hand hit writedeit, nan man ne knoweth. Darkin redethsk.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Yon nightin*: that night-in.
- *Sterresmithethe ofer*: Starforge-the over.
- *nine wecchen*: nine watches.
- *holdedantsk*: kept-they-(were), the middle voice: "were kept".

In the Vulgar:

> That night nine watches were kept over the Starforge.
>
> In the first watch the fire burned as it had always burned. Of the seven watches after it nothing is written. In the ninth watch the fire failed. Who kept the ninth watch is not written here.
>
> At lauds the ploughs still cut straight, and the walls still meant to stand. Every purposed thing kept its purpose. But none of them moved of itself again: each waited for a hand.
>
> That morning the sun rose on every world as it had always risen. Nobody had a name for it. It was called the first day of the Night, and from it the Night is counted.
>
> The Annalists did not write this leaf. Whose hand wrote it, nobody knows. It is read in the dark.

### Page two: The first year of the Compact (*Compactas first yeer*)

> *Nightthes thri hundred yeeren thurgh housantha warram makdant, hous hous agen. Ech hous hostam holdede, and nan host laweful ne was. Swa nan regol housto ne andswarde; and housantha walkerenem knightes shapin makdant, which kernem ne havdant.*
>
> *Then hallanthas moderentha firste Holiga Moderem cheosdant: Aveline Firste. Heo housanthas capteinentham Matinsward calldeit. Heo hemtö seyde: "Nan manam yuin ne cheosith. Ik shal cheosan, and ye shalith swearan."*
>
> *Heo an capteinem, Temrik Firstem, alle otheren ofer nemndeit, that he swerdthem holdethit, lightthem nevre. He hit Brighteste Sterrethe under sweardeit. Then capteinentha himtö sweardant, and hit firste Mustra was.*
>
> *Housantha hostanheorem an hostinto givdantit, wingtwain, Blew and Red, that nan captein hit al ne ledethit.*
>
> *This yeerfrem yeerentha Compactin tellantsk. Her annalistanthas hand beginneth.*
>
> *Sterrethebi given.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Nightthes thri hundred yeeren thurgh*: Night-the's three hundred years through.
- *housantha warram makdant*: houses-the war-OBJ made-they.
- *hous hous agen*: house house against.

In the Vulgar:

> For the Night's three hundred years the houses made war, house against house. Every house kept a host, and no host was lawful. So no order answered any house, and the houses built walkers in a knight's shape, which had no core.
>
> Then the Mothers of the halls chose a first Holy Mother: Aveline, the first of the name. She called the captains of the houses to Matins. She said to them: "Choose no one from among you. I shall choose, and you shall swear."
>
> She named one captain over all the others, Temrik, the first of the name, on the condition that he hold the sword and never the light. He swore it under the Brightest Star. Then the captains swore to him, and that was the first Muster.
>
> The houses gave their hosts into one Host, in two wings, Blue and Red, so that no captain should lead the whole of it.
>
> From this year the years are counted in the Compact. Here the Annalists' hand begins.
>
> Given at the Star.

**Notes.** This is the first act given at the Star, and the Annals' first dated leaf. The reigning pair's names, Temrik and Aveline, are the founders' names. The Camp makes much of that, and the Mother Hall makes nothing of it.

### Page three: The year thirty-one of the Compact (*Compactas yeer thrittig an*)

> *Temrik First Campthein deyde, and hostthe lampanhisam him bifore quenchdeit.*
>
> *Holiga Moder nine nighten thurgh Vigiliam holdede, and nan manto ne specde. Ninthe nightthes Vesperabi heo Kanam him after nemndeit: Kesrik Firstem.*
>
> *Mustrathe himtö swearde. What Mustrathe oweth, gif ne sweareth, her ne writethsk.*
>
> *Kesrik anes Brighteste Sterrethes sightinto seilde, and heimwerd cumde. Nan Kan nerer ne gangde.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Temrik First*: Temrik First.
- *Campthein deyde*: Camp-the-in died.
- *and hostthe lampanhisam*: and host-the lamps-its-OBJ.
- *him bifore quenchdeit*: him before (for his sake) quenched-it.

In the Vulgar:

> Temrik, the first of the name, died in the Camp, and the Host put out its lamps for him.
>
> The Holy Mother kept nine nights of vigil and spoke to nobody. At vespers on the ninth night she named the Khan after him: Kesrik, the first of the name.
>
> The Muster swore to him. What the Muster owes if it will not swear is not written here.
>
> Kesrik sailed once into the sight of the Brightest Star and came home. No Khan has gone nearer.

### Page four: The year two hundred and twelve of the Compact (*Compactas yeer twa hundred twelf*)

> *Baidur First, Kan, hosthisam drededeit. Mustrathein seyde: "Captein which hostthes halvam ledeth, rikethes halvam holdeth."*
>
> *Swa wingtwatha fower wingeninto deldtwask. Blew Wingthes housantha twain deldantsk, and halvathe which gangde, Gren Wing becumde. Red Wingthes housantha twain deldantsk, and halvathe which gangde, Yelow Wing becumde. Nan hous ne halvdesk.*
>
> *Holiga Moder, Aveline Othere, fower standardantham chillagwaterwith blessdeit. Yon yeer sith nan captein mare thanne hostthes fourthdel ne ledede.*
>
> *Sterrethebi given.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Baidur First, Kan*: Baidur First, Khan.
- *hosthisam drededeit*: host-his-OBJ feared-it.

In the Vulgar:

> Baidur, the first of the name, Khan, feared his own Host. He said in the Muster: "A captain who leads half the Host holds half the realm."
>
> So the two wings were dealt into four. The Blue Wing's houses were dealt in two, and the half that went became the Green Wing. The Red Wing's houses were dealt in two, and the half that went became the Yellow Wing. No house was halved.
>
> The Holy Mother, Aveline, the second of the name, blessed the four standards with star-water. Since that year no captain has led more than a quarter of the Host.
>
> Given at the Star.

### Page five: The year six hundred and three of the Compact (*Compactas yeer six hundred thri*)

> *Mustrathe Vesperes quenchstanam housanto givde, and fermenthas folk landthefram clensdesk.*
>
> *Fermentha emtidantsk, and twihousantha hemwith, alle but an hillenthain, which nevre leavdesk.*
>
> *Quenchstanthe delvdesk. Ship heimwerd mare costde thanne hit bringde.*
>
> *Yeeren after, housantha landthem again givdantit. Birchethe fieldenthaöntö again cumde, and wulfantha hit after.*
>
> *Campthebi given.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Mustrathe Vesperes quenchstanam*: Muster-the Vesper's quenchstone-OBJ.
- *housanto givde*: houses-to gave.
- *and fermenthas folk*: and farms-the's folk.
- *landthefram clensdesk*: land-the-from cleared-(was), the middle voice: "was cleared".

In the Vulgar:

> The Muster gave the quenchstone of Vesper to the houses, and the folk of the farms were cleared off the land.
>
> The farms were emptied, and the twin houses with them, all but one in the hills, which was never left.
>
> The quenchstone was dug. The ship home cost more than it brought.
>
> In the years after, the houses gave the land back. The birch came back to the fields, and the wolves after it.
>
> Given at the Camp.

### Page six: Not admitted (*Ne admitted*)

> *Ne admitted. Ritthebi ne redethsk.*
>
> *Temrik Fourth under hostthes seamrederentha Vesperes seamanam rededant. Hie seyant that ech seam twinhisem havethit, swa gif an pourungfram, and that ech seamtwa an stedewerd again renneth. Hie holdant that Sterresmithethe ther.*
>
> *Seamrederenthas tidungthe Mustrathe bifore leydesk, and Mustrathes counseilem askdant.*
>
> *Her leafthe endeth.*

The first sentence after the heading, word by word:
- *Temrik Fourth under*: Temrik Fourth under.
- *hostthes seamrederentha*: host-the's seam-readers-the.
- *Vesperes seamanam rededant*: Vesper's seams-OBJ read-they.

In the Vulgar:

> Not admitted. Not read at the rite.
>
> Under Temrik, the fourth of the name, the Host's surveyors read the seams of Vesper. They say that every seam has its twin, as if from one pouring, and that every pair runs back toward one place. They hold that the Starforge is there.
>
> The surveyors' report was laid before the Muster, and the Muster's counsel was asked.
>
> Here the leaf ends.

**Notes.** The Annalists write a leaf when a thing is done; the Mother Hall admits it to the rite. This leaf is written and not admitted, and it ends where the Muster was asked. It says that the surveyors hold the Starforge to be there, not that it is. The Annalists call the surveyors *seamrederen*, "seam-readers". Since the Division each wing's Annalists keep their own share of the archive, and no later leaf has been admitted.

## 3. The Book of Mothers (*Moderenes Boke*)

The Matriarchs' rulings and sermons. A Matriarch's words enter the book at her death, never in her life, except a sermon she gives at the Rising, which enters it the same night. That is why the book's newest page belongs to the Holy Mother who reigns now.

### Page one: Aveline the First, at the first Kindling (*Aveline Firstes prechung, firste Kindlungbi*)

> *Ó dohtren, me hearith.*
>
> *Nightthe bifore light makdesk. Makerentha holdedant that sunnathem anviltheonto bringdantit, and thingentö hit givdantit, and thingentha hit holdedantit. Yon hand gangen. Ninthe wecchethe sith nan man lightem ne smitheth.*
>
> *Now light bearethsk. Handfram handto, lampafram lampato gangeth, and nan hand hit eft ne smithethit.*
>
> *This nightin flammam flinttheüt smite. Hit makungam ne nemnith: lytelt fire, which noght ne purposeth; light anly, and light forth givethsk.*
>
> *Which childem beareth, hit lighttheinto bearethit. Which lampam beareth, lightthem othertö bearethit. Bearung moderes work. Forthy we, lytest acolitfram metil, lightthes workantham holdumit.*
>
> *Hitöf ne bostith. Hit byrthen, swa child byrthen; and forth givethsk.*
>
> *And swa wif anly moder, swa wer anly Kan. Handtwa; and nawther otheres. Swa hit holdith.*
>
> *Light bearethsk, ne makethsk.*
>
> *Beoi.*

The first sentences, word by word:
- *Ó dohtren, me hearith*: O daughters, me hear-ye.
- *Nightthe bifore*: Night-the before.
- *light makdesk*: light made-(was), the middle voice: "was made".

In the Vulgar:

> Hear me, daughters.
>
> Before the Night, light was made. The Makers held that they brought the sun down to the anvil, and gave it to things, and the things kept it. That hand is gone. Since the ninth watch nobody forges light.
>
> Now light is borne. It goes from hand to hand and from lamp to lamp, and no hand forges it again.
>
> Tonight I strike a flame from the flint. Do not call it a making. It is a small fire and purposes nothing: it is only light, and light is given on.
>
> Whoever bears a child bears it into the light. Whoever bears a lamp bears the light to another. Bearing is a mother's work. Therefore we keep the works of the light, from the least acolyte to me.
>
> Do not boast of it. It is a burden, as a child is a burden, and it is given on.
>
> And as only a woman is a Mother, so only a man is Khan. Two hands, and neither is the other's. Keep it so.
>
> Light is borne, not made.
>
> So be it.

**Notes.** *Bearan* means both "carry" and "bear a child", so *Light bearethsk* is "light is carried" and "light is born" at once; the halls' Vulgar says "light is born, not made", and the sermon is the source of the saying. The Order of the Iron Lantern's motto (*Light bearethsk, ne bidethsk*) is built the same way. Who borrowed from whom, the order and the halls do not agree. The first Kindling was struck after the Compact was given.

### Page two: Aveline the First, on the choosing (*Aveline Firstes dom, Cheosungof*)

> *Whan Kan deyeth, Holiga Moder nine nighten thurgh Vigiliam holdeth, and ninthe nightthes Vesperabi Kanam him after nemneth.*
>
> *Heo cheoseth; heo ne ledeth. Kan which heo nemnde lifehis thurgh holdeth, and nan moder hit himfrem ne takethit.*
>
> *Kinhirem heo ne shalit nemnan, ne nan manam which lightthetö serveth. Hand which blesseth handam which holdeth hirtö ne cheoseth.*
>
> *Which heo nemnde, Mustrathe himtö sweareth.*
>
> *Swa dom, sigilminwith given.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Whan Kan deyeth*: when Khan dies.
- *Holiga Moder*: Holy Mother.
- *nine nighten thurgh*: nine nights through.
- *Vigiliam holdeth*: vigil-OBJ keeps.

In the Vulgar:

> When a Khan dies, the Holy Mother keeps vigil nine nights, and at vespers on the ninth night she names the Khan after him.
>
> She chooses; she does not command. The Khan she has named holds for his whole life, and no Mother takes it from him.
>
> She shall not name her own kin, nor anyone who serves the light. The hand that blesses does not choose itself a hand that holds.
>
> Whom she has named, the Muster swears to.
>
> So it is ruled, under my seal.

### Page three: Aveline the Second, over the four standards (*Aveline Otheres blessung, fower standardanbi*)

Given at the Star in the year two hundred and twelve of the Compact (*Sterrethebi given, Compactas yeerin twa hundred twelf*).

> *Ó Blew and Red, ye twa warith. Now fower arith, and an host.*
>
> *Gren, Blewfrem boren; Yelow, Redfrem boren: ne yungere wingen arith, but same wingen, eft tellede.*
>
> *Light ech standard ofer anlike standoi. Ech wing tidenem holdoi, and stedehisin standoi, and fieldthefrem ne gangoi, til loosed.*
>
> *Fower hewen, and an oath hem under.*
>
> *Beoi.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Ó Blew and Red*: O Blue and Red.
- *ye twa warith*: you two were.

In the Vulgar:

> Blue and Red, you were two. Now you are four, and one Host.
>
> Green, born of Blue; Yellow, born of Red: you are not younger wings, but the same wings counted again.
>
> May the light stand over every standard alike. May every wing keep the hours, and stand in its place, and not go from the field until it is released.
>
> Four colours, and one oath under them.
>
> So be it.

### Page four: Hawise, on loosing (*Hawise Firstes dom, Loosungof*)

> *Oath, Sterrethe under sworen, anly Holiga Moderes namin loosethsk.*
>
> *Whan soldar hostthefram heimwerd gangeth, lampwarda him hostoaththeut, hir levewith and namhirin, Vesperabi loosethit.*
>
> *Broken oath hirtö self cumeth, and lifeoath swa.*
>
> *Regolanthas oathan, this tid bifore, sworen warant. Which this tid ne bindde, ne looseth.*
>
> *Misdede chillagwaterwith Laudesbi waschethsk; oath ne waschethsk. Holdethsk, or loosethsk.*
>
> *Swa dom, sigilminwith given.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Oath, Sterrethe under sworen*: oath, Star-the under sworn.
- *anly Holiga Moderes namin*: only Holy Mother's name-in.
- *loosethsk*: looses-(is), the middle voice: "is loosed".

In the Vulgar:

> An oath sworn under the Star is loosed only in the Holy Mother's name.
>
> When a soldier goes home from the Host, a celebrant looses him from the Host-oath at vespers, by her leave and in her name.
>
> An oath once broken comes to her in person, and so does a vow for life.
>
> The orders' oaths were sworn before this office was. What this office did not bind, it does not loose.
>
> Wrongdoing is washed in star-water at lauds; an oath is not washed. It is kept, or it is loosed.
>
> So it is ruled, under my seal.

### Page five: Emeline, on the breeders (*Emeline Firstes dom, Brederenöf*)

> *Brederentha askant gif craftheore laweful. Laweful, an grundwith anly.*
>
> *Breded wight noght ne sweareth. Which noght ne sweareth, noght breakan ne mageth; and which noght breakan ne mageth, hallantha deman ne needant.*
>
> *Forthy hallantha hit ne forbedantit; ne hit blessantit. Nan lampwarda wightem chillagwaterwith ne shal sprengan.*
>
> *Whatto wightthe, this tid ne seyeth.*
>
> *Swa dom, sigilminwith given.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Brederentha askant*: breeders-the ask.
- *gif craftheore laweful*: if craft-their (is) lawful.

In the Vulgar:

> The breeders ask whether their craft is lawful. It is lawful, on one ground only.
>
> A bred creature swears nothing. What swears nothing can break nothing, and what can break nothing the halls need not judge.
>
> So the halls do not forbid it, and they do not bless it. No celebrant shall sprinkle a creature with star-water.
>
> What the creature is for, this office does not say.
>
> So it is ruled, under my seal.

**Notes.** Emeline was the Holy Mother before Aveline III. Her ruling argues from oaths alone and says nothing of purpose: what the knights say of the breeders' craft, the halls have not answered. Hawise reigned between Aveline II and Emeline; the book runs in the order of the reigns.

### Page six: Aveline the Third, at the Rising (*Aveline Thirdes prechung, Risungbi, Moderhallthein*)

> *Vesperabi andswarum: "Lightthem takumit." Wordthem merkith.*
>
> *Highspeche, closede handto and opene handto, an wordam haveth: takan. Hallantha lerant that hit her opene handas word; and righte lerant. But wordthe mare haveth.*
>
> *Hand which taketh and holdeth, closethsk. Hand which taketh and forth giveth, ne closethsk.*
>
> *Whan righte handthem himinwerd hevumit, hit palmthewith upward hevumit, and hit ne closumit.*
>
> *Lightthem, which takum, nan hand ne holdethit: anly forth givethsk. Closede hand lightthes noght ne holdeth: darkthem holdethit, which er hitin was.*
>
> *Endungthem eke merkith. "Takum" ne seyum: sum lightem. "Takumit" seyum: lightthem, an light, which ech lampa deleth.*
>
> *Swa, Ó dohtren and sonan, handanyuram ne closith.*
>
> *Beoi.*

The first sentence, word by word:
- *Vesperabi*: vespers-at.
- *andswarum*: answer-we.
- *Lightthem takumit*: light-the-OBJ take-we-it.

In the Vulgar:

> At vespers we answer: *Lightthem takumit*, "we receive the light". Mark the word.
>
> The High Speech has one word for what the closed hand does and what the open hand does: *takan*. The halls teach that here it is the open hand's word, and they teach rightly. But the word holds more.
>
> A hand that takes and keeps closes on itself. A hand that takes and gives on does not close.
>
> When we raise the right hand to the sky, we raise it palm upward, and we do not close it.
>
> The light we take, no hand keeps: it is only given on. A closed hand holds nothing of the light. It holds the dark that was in it already.
>
> Mark the ending too. We do not say *takum*, some light. We say *takumit*: the light, the one light, which every lamp shares.
>
> So, daughters and sons, do not close your hands.
>
> So be it.

**Notes.** Aveline III gave this sermon at a Rising before the Division. It is the reading of hers most studied in the halls. Every wing has since quoted it for its own case, which it was not written to answer; the Mother Hall has said nothing about those readings, as it has said nothing about the war.

## 4. What these pages add to the canon

- The Annals' newest leaves are not read at the rite until the Mother Hall admits them.
- The Annals' first leaf is older than the Annalists, its hand unknown. It records nothing of the seven watches between the first and the ninth, and does not say who kept the ninth.
- Before the Compact, years were counted from the Quenching as **years of the Night**. The night of the Quenching itself has no year.
- The first Matriarch was **Aveline I**, and the first Khan **Temrik I**, elected at Matins. The Compact was the first act given at the Star and the Annalists' first entry.
- Temrik I died in the year 31 of the Compact; Aveline I named **Kesrik I** after nine nights of vigil, and he made the voyage to the Star. No Khan has gone nearer it than that voyage.
- The four wings: in the year 212 **Baidur I**, fearing his own Host, had the two wings dealt into four. Half of the Blue Wing's houses became the **Green Wing** and half of the Red Wing's the **Yellow Wing**; no house was split. **Aveline II** blessed the four standards, given at the Star: "not younger wings, but the same wings counted again".
- The Clearance is dated the year 603, under the Khan's seal alone ("given at the Camp").
- The Annals' newest leaf, the Seam Survey under Temrik IV, is written and **not admitted** to the rite; it ends where the Muster was asked for counsel. No leaf after it has been admitted.
- Among the Matriarchs: **Hawise**, who reigned between Aveline II and Emeline, ruled on loosing oaths; **Emeline**, Aveline III's predecessor, ruled the breeders' craft lawful on the oath argument alone.
- The Book of Mothers runs in the order of the reigns; Aveline III's page is the last because she reigns.
- Aveline I struck the first Kindling's flame. Her sermon is the source of "the light is born, not made".
- A Matriarch's words enter the Book of Mothers at her death, except a sermon given at the Rising.
- The last saying of the Book of Makings (bank the hearth, do not quench it) is read only on the Night of the Quenching.
- The Book of Makings speaks of the use of things as well as their making (the plough's furrow is the ploughman's), and of the morning: the threshold greeting of lauds.
- Aveline III's sermon on *takan* comes from before the Division and says nothing on the war, Vesper or the fire. The Silence holds.
- Nothing here answers anything in the lore's section 11.

**Grammar these pages use** (now noted in the lexicon): the delative (*-of / -öf*) also means "about, concerning", Hungarian-fashion, as in headings; the demonstratives *this* and *yon* take the article's place (*yon nightin*, "in that night"); a numeral above two takes the plural (*nine wecchen*); a numeral takes no agreement or case (*an anvilon*); a year is a cardinal after its noun, hundreds, tens and units in that order with no *and* (*Compactas yeer twa hundred twelf*); a ruler's ordinal is bare in the masculine (*Temrik First*), feminine in *-e* (*Aveline Othere*), and takes the case of the name (*Temrik Firstem*, *Aveline Firstes*).
